Rising in six stages to about 62 metres, the Step Pyramid began as a square mastaba tomb and was enlarged several times until it became a stairway to the sky.

It sits inside a 15-hectare enclosure whose limestone wall imitates a palace façade, with dummy buildings for the king's jubilee festival carved in solid stone. Beneath it lies a maze of galleries, some lined with blue faience tiles.

## The world's first monumental stone building

Before [Djoser](/pharaohs/djoser/), around 2650 BCE, Egypt's kings were buried under mastabas, low rectangular tombs of mud brick. Djoser's architect **Imhotep** built in limestone instead, cut into small blocks that still imitate the size of bricks. The result is the oldest colossal building of cut stone in the world, and the first step toward the true pyramids of the [4th Dynasty](/dynasties/4/).

## Six stages of building

Excavation, especially by the French architect Jean-Philippe Lauer, who worked at Saqqara for more than seventy years from 1926, shows how the design grew. A square mastaba about 8 metres high was extended on all sides, then lengthened to the east to cover shafts for the burials of royal family members. Then a four-stepped pyramid was built over it, and finally the pyramid was enlarged to six steps on a base of about 121 by 109 metres. The steps are made of layers of stone leaning inwards for stability, a technique used in the early pyramids.

## Under the pyramid

A central shaft, about 7 metres square and 28 metres deep, leads to a burial chamber built of granite blocks and closed by a granite plug weighing several tonnes. Around it a network of corridors and rooms, some 5.7 kilometres in all, was cut into the rock. In some, walls covered in blue-green faience tiles imitate the reed matting of a palace, and relief panels show the king performing the ritual run of the *sed* festival. Other galleries held some 40,000 stone vessels, many inscribed with the names of earlier kings. Parts of a mummy found in the chamber have been radiocarbon dated to a later period, and whether any of Djoser's body survives is uncertain.

## The enclosure

The complex covers about 15 hectares, enclosed by a wall about 10.5 metres high and 1.6 kilometres long, with bastions and panels like the façade of a royal palace. Of its fourteen gateways, only one, in the south-east corner, is real. It opens into a corridor of columns carved to look like bundled reeds, the first stone columns in Egyptian architecture.

Inside are the great south court, with stones marking the course of the jubilee run, the *sed* festival court lined with solid dummy chapels, and the Houses of the North and of the South, which may represent the two halves of the kingdom. The **South Tomb**, under the enclosure's south wall, repeats the pyramid's burial chamber and blue-tiled galleries on a smaller scale; its purpose is still debated. On the north side of the pyramid, the *serdab*, a closed stone chamber, held a seated statue of the king that looked out through two eye-holes toward the northern stars.

## Restoration

An earthquake in 1992 damaged the pyramid, and a restoration project completed in 2020 consolidated its walls and burial chamber, which were then reopened to visitors.
