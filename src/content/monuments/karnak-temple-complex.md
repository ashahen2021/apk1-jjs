Karnak was the religious heart of the New Kingdom, the "most select of places" where Amun-Ra was worshipped. Almost every pharaoh from the Middle Kingdom to the Ptolemies added a pylon, chapel or obelisk.

Its **Great Hypostyle Hall**, completed under Seti I and [Ramesses II](/pharaohs/ramesses-ii/), contains 134 columns; the twelve central ones stand about 21 metres high. [Hatshepsut](/pharaohs/hatshepsut/)'s surviving obelisk is one of the tallest ancient obelisks still standing.

## A city of temples

Karnak is not a single building but a group of temple precincts on the east bank of the Nile at Thebes, the city the Egyptians called Waset, covering more than 100 hectares. The largest is the precinct of **Amun-Ra**, king of the gods. To the south lies the precinct of his consort, the goddess Mut, and to the north that of the war god Montu. Amun's son, the moon god Khonsu, had his own temple within the main precinct. The ancient name of Karnak was *Ipet-isut*, "the most select of places".

## Two thousand years of building

The oldest surviving building is a small limestone chapel of Senusret I of the 12th Dynasty, the White Chapel, reconstructed from blocks found inside a later pylon. When Thebes became the capital of the New Kingdom after the expulsion of the Hyksos, its kings poured the wealth of their empire into Amun's temple. [Thutmose III](/pharaohs/thutmose-iii/) built his festival hall, the Akh-menu, and carved his Annals near the sanctuary. Amenhotep III built the third pylon, Seti I and Ramesses II the hypostyle hall, the Kushite king [Taharqa](/pharaohs/taharqa/) a colonnade in the first court, and the Ptolemies gateways and chapels. The great first pylon, about 113 metres wide, was never finished.

The temple grew along two axes: from west to east, toward the river, and from north to south, toward the temple of Mut and, beyond it, Luxor Temple, about three kilometres away and connected by an avenue of sphinxes.

## The Great Hypostyle Hall

The hall covers about 5,000 square metres. The twelve columns of its central aisle have capitals shaped like open papyrus flowers; the 122 lower columns on either side have closed papyrus-bud capitals. The difference in height left room for clerestory windows with stone grilles that let light into the dim interior. Every surface is carved: the inner walls with rituals and processions, the outer north wall with Seti I's wars in the Levant, and the outer south wall with the Battle of Kadesh and the text of the peace treaty of Ramesses II.

## The sacred lake and the festivals

A rectangular sacred lake, still filled with water, supplied the priests for their purification and the temple for its rites. In the Opet festival, held each year in the flood season, the statues of Amun, Mut and Khonsu were carried in their gilded boats from Karnak to Luxor Temple, where the king's divine power was renewed. In the Beautiful Festival of the Valley, Amun crossed the river to visit the temples of the west bank, among them the [Temple of Hatshepsut](/monuments/temple-of-hatshepsut/).

## Rediscovery

In 1903 Georges Legrain found in a single pit in the court of the seventh pylon, the "Karnak Cachette", some 750 stone statues and 17,000 bronzes buried by priests in the Ptolemaic period. Excavation and restoration continue today, led by the Franco-Egyptian Centre for the Study of the Temples of Karnak.

## Visiting Karnak today

Karnak is on the east bank at Luxor, about three kilometres north of Luxor Temple. Since 2021 the two temples are linked again by the restored **Avenue of Sphinxes**, which you can walk in part. Karnak is large enough to need at least half a day. A route that shows its history in order:

- **Start at the first pylon**, the unfinished gateway of the last native kings, and look for the mud-brick construction ramp still leaning against its inner face.
- **Find the single column** of Taharqa's kiosk in the first court, the last standing of ten.
- **Walk the Great Hypostyle Hall slowly.** Cleaning in recent years has revealed paint on the column capitals and the architraves high above you.
- **See Hatshepsut's obelisk** and, nearby, the fallen top of its twin, which lies by the sacred lake.
- **Visit the sacred lake** and the giant granite scarab of Amenhotep III beside it.
- **Do not skip the Open Air Museum** in the north-west corner. Senusret I's White Chapel, the oldest complete building at Karnak, and Hatshepsut's Red Chapel have been rebuilt there from blocks found inside later pylons.

## What we know, and what is still argued

- **Established:** Karnak grew over about two thousand years, from the Middle Kingdom to the Ptolemies, around the cult of Amun-Ra.
- **Established:** the Great Hypostyle Hall was decorated under Seti I and Ramesses II, and its outer walls carry Seti's wars and Ramesses' Battle of Kadesh.
- **Likely:** the first pylon was begun under the 30th Dynasty, the last native dynasty, and left unfinished.
- **Debated:** how old the sacred site is. Traces of buildings older than Senusret I suggest worship here before the Middle Kingdom, but little survives.
- **Debated:** how the colossal columns and roof blocks were raised. Ramps of mud brick and sand are the usual answer, and the construction ramp left at the first pylon supports it.
