Four seated colossi of [Ramesses II](/pharaohs/ramesses-ii/), each about 20 metres high, guard the entrance of the Great Temple. A smaller temple beside it is dedicated to Hathor and to Queen **Nefertari**.

The temple axis was aligned so that twice a year the rising sun reaches the sanctuary and lights the statues within. Between 1964 and 1968 an international UNESCO campaign cut both temples into blocks and rebuilt them on higher ground above the rising waters of the Aswan High Dam.

## A message in stone to Nubia

The temples were cut into two sandstone cliffs on the west bank of the Nile in Lower Nubia, some 280 kilometres south of Aswan, in a project that took about twenty years from around 1264 BCE. Nubia was a source of gold and a frontier province, and the temples were a declaration of Egyptian power to anyone travelling on the river, as well as monuments to the king's own divinity.

## The Great Temple

The façade's four colossi show the king seated, wearing the double crown; small figures of his mother, his wife Nefertari and some of his children stand between their legs. Above the entrance, a figure of the falcon-headed sun god Ra-Horakhty stands in a niche. Inside, a pillared hall about 18 metres deep is lined with eight statues of the king as Osiris, and its walls show his wars, above all the Battle of Kadesh in his fifth year, with the Egyptian camp, the Hittite chariots and the king charging alone in his chariot.

At the back, some 60 metres into the rock, four statues sit in the sanctuary: Ra-Horakhty, the deified Ramesses II, Amun-Ra and Ptah. On about 22 February and 22 October, the rising sun shines down the length of the temple and lights three of them. Ptah, a god connected with the underworld, remains in shadow. The dates today are one day later than in antiquity because of the temple's move.

## The temple of Nefertari

The smaller temple honours Hathor and Nefertari. On its façade, six standing statues about 10 metres tall show the king and the queen at the same scale, an unusual honour for a queen. Inside, Nefertari is shown crowned by Hathor and Isis. Her tomb, [QV66](/tombs/qv66-nefertari/), is the most beautiful in the Valley of the Queens.

## Rediscovery and rescue

By the 19th century sand had buried most of the façade. The Swiss traveller Johann Ludwig Burckhardt saw the top of the colossi in 1813, and Giovanni Belzoni dug his way inside in 1817. In the 1950s the planned Aswan High Dam threatened to drown the temples under Lake Nasser. In a campaign supported by some fifty countries, engineers cut the temples into more than a thousand blocks, some weighing up to 30 tonnes, and reassembled them about 65 metres higher and 200 metres back from the river, inside an artificial hill built over a concrete dome. The rescue led directly to the idea of World Heritage, and the temples became part of the UNESCO site "Nubian Monuments from Abu Simbel to Philae" in 1979.
