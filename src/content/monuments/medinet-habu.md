The best-preserved mortuary temple in Thebes, Medinet Habu was built for [Ramesses III](/pharaohs/ramesses-iii/), surrounded by massive mud-brick walls and entered through a fortified gate modelled on a Syrian fortress.

Its outer walls carry the famous reliefs of Ramesses III's victory over the **Sea Peoples**, and inside, painted ceilings and deep-cut hieroglyphs still preserve much of their original colour.

## A sacred place before Ramesses

The site was holy long before Ramesses III. Thebans believed that a mound here, called Djeme, was one of the places where creation began and where the primeval gods were buried. Hatshepsut and Thutmose III built a small temple to Amun on the spot, and it remained in use, enlarged by later rulers down to the Ptolemies and Romans, beside the great temple of Ramesses.

## The temple of "millions of years"

Ramesses III's temple, called "the Mansion of Millions of Years of King Usermaatra Meryamun, united with eternity", follows the plan of the Ramesseum built by his hero [Ramesses II](/pharaohs/ramesses-ii/) about a mile to the north. Two great pylons lead through open courts to a hypostyle hall and the sanctuaries of Amun, Mut and Khonsu. A royal palace was built against the south side of the first court, with a "window of appearances" from which the king could show himself to his court and reward his officials.

The enclosure wall, about 18 metres high, made the whole complex a fortress. Its eastern gateway, the **Migdol** or High Gate, imitates the towered forts of the Levant; its upper rooms are decorated with scenes of the king at leisure with the women of his household.

## The wars on the walls

The outer face of the north wall shows the king's campaigns against the Libyans and the Sea Peoples in vast scenes. The sea battle, in which Egyptian ships close with enemy vessels whose prows end in birds' heads, is the only large-scale depiction of a naval battle from the ancient Near East. Elsewhere scribes are shown counting the severed hands of the dead. The long inscriptions beside the scenes give the names of the invading peoples and the king's account of the danger Egypt faced.

## Later life

When order broke down at the end of the 20th Dynasty, the people of western Thebes, including the tomb builders of Deir el-Medina, moved inside the walls for safety, and the town of Djeme grew up within the enclosure. It was still inhabited in the Christian period, when a church was built in the second court, and was abandoned only around the 9th century CE. In the 20th century the University of Chicago's Epigraphic Survey, based at Chicago House in Luxor, recorded every wall in precise facsimile drawings, a project begun in 1924.

Medinet Habu lies at the southern end of the Theban west bank, a short distance from the Valley of the Queens and Deir el-Medina. See it in its landscape on the [Valley of the Kings 3D map](/tombs/valley-of-the-kings-map/).
