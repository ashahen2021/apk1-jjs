Designed, according to tradition, by the official **Senenmut**, the temple rises in three colonnaded terraces set against a sheer limestone cliff, next to the older temple of [Mentuhotep II](/pharaohs/mentuhotep-ii/).

Its reliefs record the divine birth of [Hatshepsut](/pharaohs/hatshepsut/) and the celebrated expedition to the land of **Punt**, showing ships loaded with incense trees, ebony and exotic animals.

## The "Holiest of Holies"

The Egyptians called it *Djeser-Djeseru*, "Holiest of Holies". It stands in the bay of cliffs at Deir el-Bahari, on the west bank opposite Karnak, on an axis aimed across the river toward the great temple of Amun. Each year, in the Beautiful Festival of the Valley, the statue of Amun was carried from Karnak across the Nile to visit the temples of the west bank, and Hatshepsut's temple was its main destination.

## Three terraces

A long processional avenue lined with sphinxes led to the temple. Ramps climb from the lower court to the middle terrace and then to the upper terrace, each fronted by a colonnade of square pillars. On the middle terrace, the colonnade to the south carries the Punt reliefs and the one to the north the story of the queen's divine birth, in which Amun takes the form of her father to visit her mother, Queen Ahmose, and the ram-headed god Khnum shapes the child on his potter's wheel. At the ends of this terrace are a chapel of Hathor, with columns topped by the goddess's face, and a chapel of Anubis.

The upper terrace was fronted by large statues of Hatshepsut in the form of Osiris, and behind its courtyard the sanctuary of Amun is cut into the cliff. Chapels here were also dedicated to the sun god and to the cult of the queen and her father, Thutmose I.

## Senenmut

Senenmut, steward of Amun and tutor of the queen's daughter Neferura, was the most powerful official of the reign. Small figures of him were carved behind the doors of chapels in the temple, where they would be hidden when the doors stood open, and he began a tomb for himself beneath the temple's forecourt.

## Damage, rediscovery and restoration

Late in Thutmose III's reign Hatshepsut's figures and names were cut out, and her statues were smashed and thrown into a quarry nearby, where Herbert Winlock's excavations for the Metropolitan Museum of Art found the pieces in the 1920s. Akhenaten's agents later attacked the images of Amun, and in the Christian era a monastery was built on the upper terrace, which gave the site its Arabic name, Deir el-Bahari, "monastery of the north". Since 1961 a Polish–Egyptian mission has worked to restore the temple.

Nearby, in 1881, a cliff tomb known as DB320 was found to hold the mummies of more than fifty kings, queens and nobles, hidden there by priests for safety.
