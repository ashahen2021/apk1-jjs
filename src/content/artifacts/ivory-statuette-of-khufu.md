Khufu built the Great Pyramid, the largest pyramid ever made, yet the only complete three-dimensional image certainly identified as him is a tiny ivory figure about 7.5 cm tall. It is one of the great ironies of Egyptian archaeology.

## What it shows

The king sits on a block throne with a low back. He wears the Red Crown of Lower Egypt and a short kilt, and holds a flail across his chest in his right hand. His Horus name, Medjedu, is carved on the side of the throne; this inscription is what identifies the figure as Khufu. Although small, the face is carefully modelled, with a firm jaw and a strong expression.

## A head found in the spoil

The statuette was found in 1903 by the British archaeologist Flinders Petrie in the area of the temple of Khentyimentiu, the early god of the dead, at Abydos. The body came to light first, without its head. Realising that the break was fresh and that the head must be in the excavated earth, Petrie had the spoil heaps sieved; the head was found about three weeks later. It is now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo (JE 36143).

## A puzzle of date

Because the statuette was found far from Giza and is so small, some scholars have suggested that it is not a work of Khufu's own time but a later commemorative image, made centuries after his death, when Khufu was honoured as an ancestor and his cult continued. Others accept it as a work of the Fourth Dynasty. Either way, it remains the only certain sculpture of the king in the round; other heads that may portray him cannot be identified with certainty.

Its modest size also fits the picture painted by later traditions. Ancient Greek writers such as Herodotus remembered Khufu (Cheops) as a harsh ruler, a reputation for which there is no contemporary evidence, but which shows how the memory of the great pyramid builder took on a life of its own.
