The painted bust of Nefertiti, the Great Royal Wife of Akhenaten, is one of the most famous works of ancient art. It stands 48 cm tall and weighs about 20 kg. It has no inscription, but the queen is identified with certainty by her distinctive flat-topped blue crown, which she wears in other, clearly labelled images.

## The sculptor's workshop

The bust was found on 6 December 1912 at Amarna, the city Akhenaten built for the god Aten, by a German team led by Ludwig Borchardt and funded by the German Oriental Society. It lay in the ruins of the workshop of the royal sculptor Thutmose, together with other unfinished portraits of the royal family. It is usually dated to about 1345 BCE.

Its purpose is not known for certain. The most widely accepted view is that it was a master model, kept in the workshop as a reference for other official portraits of the queen.

## How it was made

The bust has a limestone core covered with layers of painted stucco. The face is almost perfectly symmetrical. The right eye has an inlaid iris of quartz, painted black and fixed with beeswax; the left eye was never inlaid. No missing inlay was found in the workshop despite a search and a large reward, and many scholars think the eye was simply left unfinished, perhaps for teaching.

CT scans made in 1992 and 2006 revealed a carved face in the limestone core beneath the stucco, with slight creases around the mouth and cheeks and a bump on the nose. The sculptor had smoothed these with the outer layer, a glimpse of how carefully the final, idealised face was built up. Analysis of the pigments, first published in 1923 and confirmed in 1982, matched them to those used in the Eighteenth Dynasty, and the scans support the bust's authenticity against occasional claims that it is a modern fake.

## Berlin and the question of return

The bust has been in Germany since 1913. Hidden during the Second World War, it was recovered by the US Army in 1945, displayed in Wiesbaden, then in West Berlin, and since 2009 again in the Neues Museum, where it is seen by hundreds of thousands of visitors a year.

Egypt has asked for its return since it was first shown to the public in 1924. Egyptian officials argue that it was taken out of Egypt by misleading the inspectors during the division of finds; German authorities point to the protocol signed with the Egyptian Antiquities Service in 1913 and say the bust is too fragile to travel. The debate continues.
