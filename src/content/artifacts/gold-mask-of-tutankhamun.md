The gold funerary mask of Tutankhamun is perhaps the best-known object from ancient Egypt. It covered the head and shoulders of the king's mummy inside the innermost of three nested coffins in his tomb, KV62, in the Valley of the Kings.

## Discovery

Howard Carter's team found the tomb in 1922, but it took until 28 October 1925 to open the innermost coffin. Carter described what he saw in his diary: a neatly wrapped mummy "with golden mask of sad but tranquil expression, symbolizing Osiris". In December 1925 the mask was taken to the Egyptian Museum in Cairo. Today it is displayed at the Grand Egyptian Museum.

## Gold, glass and stone

The mask is 54 cm tall, 39.3 cm wide and 49 cm deep, and weighs about 10.2 kg. It is made of two layers of high-carat gold, 1.5–3 mm thick. Analysis in 2007 showed that the surface carries a very thin layer of two gold alloys: a paler one for the face and neck and a richer one for the rest.

The king wears the striped nemes headcloth; the blue stripes are glass. On his brow are the vulture of Nekhbet and the cobra of Wadjet, the protective goddesses of Upper and Lower Egypt. The eyes are quartz with obsidian pupils, set in lapis lazuli, and carnelian, amazonite, turquoise and faience also decorate the mask. A triple-string necklace of gold and blue faience beads belongs with it but is usually removed for display.

## A spell for protection

Ten vertical and two horizontal lines of hieroglyphs on the back and shoulders carry a protective spell, later known as Chapter 151 of the Book of the Dead, which first appeared on masks in the Middle Kingdom. It links each part of the face to a god: the eyes to the day and night boats of the sun, the eyebrows to the Ennead, the forehead to Anubis, the nape to Horus. The mask presents the king as Osiris, ruler of the dead, while the gold and lapis lazuli recall the body of the sun god Ra.

## Beard and debate

The plaited beard, about 2.5 kg, had come away from the mask by the time it was found and was fixed back in 1944. In 2014 it came off again during cleaning and was glued back hastily with epoxy; a German–Egyptian team removed the glue and reattached it with beeswax in 2015.

Some scholars, notably Nicholas Reeves, have suggested that parts of the mask were first made for Neferneferuaten, a ruler who reigned just before Tutankhamun, and later given his face and names. The conservator who restored the mask in 2015 found no evidence of a separate face or altered names, and the question remains open.

## Seeing the mask today

Since the Grand Egyptian Museum opened fully in November 2025, the mask has been displayed in the museum's **Tutankhamun Galleries** at Giza, near the pyramids. For the first time since the discovery, the whole burial equipment of KV62 is shown together, so the mask can be seen alongside the coffins, shrines and chariots that surrounded it.

- **Look at the ears.** They are pierced. The holes were covered with small gold discs when the mask was found, and earrings were usually shown on images of princes and women, not on adult kings.
- **Walk round to the back** to see the ten lines of the protective spell, which most photographs never show.
- **Then see the tomb itself.** The mummy is still in KV62, in the Valley of the Kings, and you can explore its chambers in 3D on our [KV62 page](/tombs/kv62-tutankhamun/).

## What we know, and what is still argued

- **Established:** the mask was found on Tutankhamun's mummy in 1925, and it is made of two sheets of high-carat gold with stone and glass inlays.
- **Established:** the beard came off in 2014, was badly glued, and was correctly reattached with beeswax in 2015.
- **Debated:** whether the mask was first made for another ruler, probably Neferneferuaten, and adapted for Tutankhamun. The 2015 conservation found no clear sign of a replaced face.
- **Open question:** why the ears are pierced. It may reflect the king's youth, or that parts of the mask were made for someone else.
