The Narmer Palette is a shield-shaped slab of fine dark grey-green siltstone (greywacke), about 64 cm tall, carved in low relief on both sides. In form it belongs to the family of cosmetic palettes on which eye paint was ground, but its size and decoration show that it was a ceremonial object, made to be dedicated in a temple. It dates from about the 31st century BCE and carries some of the earliest hieroglyphic signs ever found.

## The king on both faces

At the top of each side, two cow heads with human faces, usually identified as the goddess Bat, flank a serekh, the frame that holds a royal name. Inside it, a catfish (*nꜥr*) and a chisel (*mr*) spell the name Narmer.

On one side the king, wearing the tall White Crown of Upper Egypt, raises a mace to strike a kneeling captive he holds by the hair. Above the captive, a falcon, the god Horus, perches on papyrus plants and holds a rope tied to a human head: a picture of the king's power over the land of the papyrus.

On the other side Narmer wears the Red Crown of Lower Egypt and walks in procession behind standard bearers towards rows of decapitated enemies. Below, two men restrain a pair of serpopards, mythical creatures with leopards' bodies and long serpent necks. Their intertwined necks frame the round hollow where cosmetics would have been ground. At the bottom, a bull, another image of the king, breaks down the walls of a town and tramples a fallen foe.

## Found at Nekhen

The palette was discovered in 1897–98 by the British archaeologists James Quibell and Frederick Green in the so-called Main Deposit of the temple at Hierakonpolis (ancient Nekhen), together with ceremonial maceheads, including the Narmer Macehead and the Scorpion Macehead. The excavators' records of exactly where it lay are unclear. It is now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo (JE 32169).

## Event or ideology?

Because Narmer appears wearing both crowns, the palette was long read as a record of the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt. Scholars still debate this. Some see a specific victory, perhaps over a Delta stronghold; others, such as Nicholas Millet, argue that it records the events of the year in which it was dedicated; others again read it as a statement of the ideology of kingship rather than a historical report. Either way, it set conventions that Egyptian royal art kept for three thousand years: the king shown larger than everyone else, and the pose of the king smiting his enemies.
