The Younger Memnon is the upper part of a colossal seated statue of Ramesses II. It is one of a pair of granite colossi that flanked a doorway of the Ramesseum, the king's memorial temple in western Thebes. The head and upper torso are in the British Museum; the rest of the statue remains in Egypt, and the head of its partner still lies at the Ramesseum.

## A carving that uses the stone

The fragment is about 2.7 m high and 2 m across the shoulders, and weighs some 7.25 tonnes. It was cut from a single block of granite with two colours, and the sculptors placed the head in one colour and the body in the other. The king wears the nemes headcloth with a cobra on his brow, and his eyes look down a little more than usual, towards those approaching the temple.

## From Thebes to London

French soldiers of Napoleon's expedition tried and failed to move the statue in 1798–99; the hole on the right of the chest is said to date from that attempt. In 1815 the British Consul General, Henry Salt, hired the engineer and adventurer Giovanni Belzoni to bring it to Britain, an idea suggested by the explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt. In 1816 Belzoni had it dragged on wooden rollers to the bank of the Nile by hundreds of workers, and it reached England in 1818. The British Museum acquired it from Salt in 1821, and in 1834 it was installed in the new Egyptian Sculpture Gallery with the help of the Royal Engineers.

In London it was named after the "Memnonium", the classical name of the Ramesseum. The name confuses it with the Colossi of Memnon, two statues of Amenhotep III that classical visitors associated with the hero Memnon.

## "Ozymandias"

The statue's arrival coincided with a friendly competition between the poets Percy Bysshe Shelley and Horace Smith, who both published sonnets called "Ozymandias", a Greek form of Usermaatre, one of Ramesses II's names. Shelley's poem, with its "colossal wreck" in the desert, became one of the most famous in English. The Younger Memnon was also featured as object 20 in the BBC Radio 4 series *A History of the World in 100 Objects* (2010).
