This nearly life-size statue, about 195 cm tall, shows Hatshepsut seated on a throne as king of Egypt. It comes from her memorial temple at Deir el-Bahari in western Thebes and is now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

## A woman as pharaoh

Hatshepsut, the daughter of Thutmose I, first acted as regent for her young stepson Thutmose III and then took the full titles of a king, ruling for about two decades in the 15th century BCE. Her statues show how she and her artists solved a problem that Egyptian art had never faced: how to portray a female king.

Here she wears the striped nemes headcloth and the short kilt of a king, the traditional regalia. Yet the face is delicate and the body slender, with a hint of a woman's form, and the inscriptions on the throne use her royal names. Many scholars see this as an early statue of her reign. Later statues show her with a fully masculine body and a false beard, following the conventional image of a king more closely.

## Smashed and buried

Some two decades after her death, during the reign of Thutmose III, Hatshepsut's statues were deliberately broken and her images and names were removed from many monuments. The pieces of her statues from Deir el-Bahari were dumped in quarries and pits near the temple.

In the 1920s the Metropolitan Museum's Egyptian Expedition, led by Herbert Winlock, excavated these deposits and recovered thousands of fragments. Under the division of finds then in use, part of the material went to New York and part stayed in Egypt, and the pieces were fitted back together. This statue was reassembled from fragments, and the joins can still be seen.

## Why the statues were destroyed

The attack on Hatshepsut's memory was once explained as revenge by Thutmose III. Because it came so long after her death, most historians now think it was a political measure: removing a female king from the official record, possibly to secure the succession of Thutmose III's son, Amenhotep II. Her temple at Deir el-Bahari, one of the masterpieces of Egyptian architecture, survived, and her statues, pieced together again, now stand in museums in Cairo and New York.
