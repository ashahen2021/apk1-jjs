Born Amenhotep IV, a son of Amenhotep III and Queen Tiye, he changed his name to **Akhenaten**, "effective for the Aten", and made the sun disc the centre of state religion. The temples of Amun were closed and the god's name was hacked from monuments across the country.

## From Thebes to a new city

The reign began conventionally at Thebes. East of Karnak the young king built temples to the Aten, decorated in a strikingly new style and assembled from small, standard sandstone blocks, now called *talatat*, that one worker could carry. After his death tens of thousands of these blocks were reused as fill inside the pylons of later kings at Karnak, which is how so many survived.

Around his fifth year the king changed his name and founded a new capital on an empty stretch of the east bank in Middle Egypt: **Akhetaten**, "Horizon of the Aten", today called Amarna. Boundary stelae carved into the surrounding cliffs record his vow never to move the city's limits. Palaces, open-air temples, workshops and the villas of officials went up within a few years.

## A new religion and a new art

The Aten was shown not as a human or animal figure but as a disc whose rays end in small hands, some holding the ankh, the sign of life, to the noses of the king and queen. The *Great Hymn to the Aten*, carved in the Amarna tomb of the official Ay, praises the sun as the single creator of all lands and peoples.

Royal art changed with the religion. Akhenaten was shown with a long face, narrow shoulders and a full belly, and the royal family appears in scenes of unusual intimacy, the king kissing his daughters or holding them on his knee. His chief queen, Nefertiti, is as prominent as the king in many scenes. Her painted limestone bust, found in 1912 in the workshop of the sculptor Thutmose at Amarna, is now one of the best-known works of ancient art: see the [bust of Nefertiti](/artifacts/bust-of-nefertiti/).

## The Amarna letters

In 1887 some 380 clay tablets were found at Amarna. Written in cuneiform, mostly in Akkadian, the diplomatic language of the age, they are letters between the Egyptian court and the great kings of Babylon, Assyria, Mitanni and Hatti, and the rulers of Egypt's vassal cities in Canaan and Syria. Great kings ask for gold and arrange royal marriages; vassals such as Rib-Hadda of Byblos send urgent appeals for troops. Together they form the earliest large archive of international diplomacy.

## Death and erasure

Akhenaten died around his seventeenth regnal year. The succession that followed is one of the most debated questions in Egyptology: one or two short-lived rulers, Smenkhkare and Neferneferuaten, reigned before the boy Tutankhaten came to the throne and changed his name to [Tutankhamun](/pharaohs/tutankhamun/). Amarna was abandoned, and the old cults were restored.

Later kings treated the period as if it had never happened. The king lists at Abydos pass from Amenhotep III straight to Horemheb, and a legal text from the reign of Ramesses II refers to Akhenaten only as "the enemy of Akhetaten". A mummy found in tomb KV55 in the Valley of the Kings was shown by DNA analysis in 2010 to be the father of Tutankhamun and a son of Amenhotep III; many scholars identify it as Akhenaten, though this is still argued.
