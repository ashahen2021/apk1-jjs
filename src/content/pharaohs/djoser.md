Known in his own time by his Horus name **Netjerikhet**, Djoser ruled from Memphis at the dawn of the Old Kingdom. His reign is remembered for one revolutionary idea: building for eternity in stone rather than mud brick. The name Djoser itself is known mainly from later sources, including graffiti left by New Kingdom visitors to his monument more than a thousand years after his death.

## The first pyramid

His vizier and architect **Imhotep** began with a square, flat-topped stone tomb of the kind called a mastaba, then enlarged it several times. In its final form six platforms of decreasing size are stacked on top of one another, rising to about 62 metres: the [Step Pyramid](/monuments/step-pyramid-of-djoser/) at Saqqara, the earliest large building in the world made entirely of cut stone.

Beneath it a shaft about 28 metres deep leads to a burial chamber built of granite, surrounded by a maze of galleries. Some of these were lined with blue-green faience tiles imitating reed matting, and relief panels show the king running the ritual course of the *sed* festival, the jubilee that renewed his power. Tens of thousands of stone vessels were stored in other galleries.

## A city for the king's spirit

Around the pyramid lies a rectangular enclosure some 545 by 278 metres, walled in fine limestone panelled like a palace façade. Only one of its many gateways is real. Inside, rows of dummy chapels for the jubilee court, solid buildings with false doors, translate into stone the reed and timber shrines of earlier ceremonies. In a sealed chamber on the pyramid's north side, called a *serdab*, a seated statue of the king looked out through two small holes; the original is now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo, with a cast in its place.

The complex set the pattern that the kings of the [4th Dynasty](/dynasties/4/) would follow and transform at Meidum, Dahshur and Giza. To see how far that idea was taken within a century, try the [Pyramid Builder Lab](/play/pyramid-lab/), which models the building of the Great Pyramid.

## Imhotep and Djoser's later fame

Imhotep's name and titles, including "chancellor of the king of Lower Egypt" and "high priest of Heliopolis", appear on the base of a statue of Djoser found at Saqqara, a rare case of an architect named on a royal monument. Later generations remembered him as a sage and writer; by the Late Period he was worshipped as a god of medicine, and the Greeks identified him with Asclepius.

Djoser too grew in later memory. On Sehel Island near Aswan, an inscription carved under the Ptolemies, some two and a half thousand years after his reign, tells how a seven-year famine under Djoser ended when the king honoured Khnum, god of the cataract. This *Famine Stela* says more about the priests who carved it than about Djoser, but it shows that he was still remembered as a founder.
