Son of Sneferu, the most prolific pyramid builder in Egyptian history, Khufu inherited both the know-how and the organisation to attempt something without precedent. The result was the **[Great Pyramid](/monuments/great-pyramid/)**, originally about 146 metres tall and built from roughly 2.3 million blocks.

## The king behind the pyramid

His full name was Khnum-Khufu, "Khnum protects me", after the ram-headed creator god. The Turin king list, compiled more than a thousand years later, gives him a reign of 23 years; contemporary records of cattle counts suggest at least 26 or 27. For the king who built the largest pyramid ever made, remarkably little of him survives in person. The only complete statue that can be securely identified as Khufu is a seated ivory figure just 7.5 centimetres tall, found by Flinders Petrie at Abydos in 1903: see the [ivory statuette of Khufu](/artifacts/ivory-statuette-of-khufu/).

## Merer's logbook

Papyri found at Wadi el-Jarf on the Red Sea coast in 2013, the oldest inscribed papyri known, record an official named **Merer** and his team of about forty men shipping limestone from the quarries of Tura across the Nile to Giza, in the last years of Khufu's reign. The logbook notes trips made, nights spent at harbours and stone delivered to "Akhet-Khufu", the name of the pyramid. It is a rare first-hand glimpse of the workforce behind the monument, and the evidence on which the [Pyramid Builder Lab](/play/pyramid-lab/) bases its river transport.

## A complex for eternity

Khufu's pyramid stood within a complex: a mortuary temple on its east side, of which only a basalt pavement remains, a causeway to a valley temple now under the modern town, three small pyramids for queens, and fields of stone mastaba tombs for his family and officials laid out in neat streets. Pits beside the pyramid held full-size boats. In 1954 Kamal el-Mallakh found one of them still sealed, containing a cedar boat about 43.5 metres long in some 1,200 pieces; reassembled, it is now displayed at the Grand Egyptian Museum. See the [Giza Plateau](/monuments/giza-plateau/) for the whole site.

## Remembered as a tyrant

Later tradition was not kind to him. Herodotus, who called him "Cheops", described a king who closed the temples and forced his people into labour. The Westcar Papyrus, written down in the Middle Kingdom, shows him listening to tales of magicians and wanting to see a magician restore a beheaded prisoner. Neither portrait is evidence for the man himself, but both show how the scale of his pyramid shaped his reputation. His sons Djedefre and [Khafre](/pharaohs/khafre/) succeeded him in turn.
