Nectanebo II, in Egyptian **Nakhthorhebyt**, was the last native king of an independent ancient Egypt. He came to power by deposing his uncle Teos and spent much of his reign preparing for a Persian invasion. At the same time he sponsored a remarkable wave of temple building and sacred animal cults.

## A throne taken on campaign

The 30th Dynasty ruled an Egypt that had won back its independence from Persia only a few decades before. Around 360 BCE King Teos led an army into Phoenicia to strike at the Persian empire, with Greek mercenaries and the aged Spartan king Agesilaus among his commanders. To pay for the war he had imposed heavy taxes, including on the temples. While he was abroad, his nephew Nectanebo, who commanded part of the army, was proclaimed king with the support of the troops and of Agesilaus. Teos fled to the Persian court.

## Holding off Persia

For almost two decades Nectanebo II kept Egypt free. Around 351 BCE his generals beat back a Persian attack. In 343 BCE the Persian king Artaxerxes III returned with a much larger force. Greek mercenaries on the Persian side broke through at Pelusium, on the eastern edge of the Delta, and the Egyptian fortresses surrendered one after another. Nectanebo withdrew to Memphis, then fled south, possibly to Nubia, and disappears from the record. Egypt would not be ruled again by a native pharaoh.

## A king who built for the gods

Inscriptions of Nectanebo II are known from around a hundred sites, the result of an intense building programme meant, it seems, to win the favour of the gods for a kingdom under threat. He began the great granite temple of Isis at Behbeit el-Hagar in the Delta, and the granite shrine that still stands in the sanctuary of the temple of Edfu bears his name. He supported the cults of sacred animals, such as the Apis bull at Saqqara and the Buchis bull at Armant, which flourished in this period. Statues show the king standing small and protected between the feet of a great Horus falcon, an image of the king under the god's protection.

## A sarcophagus and a legend

His tomb was probably prepared at Sebennytos or Memphis, but he was never buried in it. His massive green stone sarcophagus later turned up in Alexandria, reused as a bath in a mosque. When it was taken to the British Museum in 1802 it was thought to be the coffin of Alexander the Great, until its hieroglyphs were read and found to name Nectanebo.

Legend gave him a stranger end. The *Alexander Romance*, a popular Greek story of later centuries, tells how Nectanebo fled to Macedon disguised as a magician and became the true father of Alexander the Great, so that Egypt's conqueror was secretly the son of its last pharaoh.
