Born Tutankhaten, probably a son of [Akhenaten](/pharaohs/akhenaten/), the young king changed his name to **Tutankhamun** as the court returned to Thebes and Memphis and the old gods were restored.

His short reign would be a footnote if not for **[KV62](/tombs/kv62-tutankhamun/)**. Discovered in November 1922, it was the only royal tomb in the Valley of the Kings found largely intact, and its treasures, the [gold mask](/artifacts/gold-mask-of-tutankhamun/) above all, shaped the modern image of ancient Egypt.

## Family

DNA tests published in 2010 showed that his father was the man buried in tomb KV55, very probably Akhenaten, and his mother a woman known as the "Younger Lady" from tomb KV35, who was also her husband's full sister. Tutankhamun married Ankhesenpaaten, a daughter of Akhenaten and Nefertiti, who also changed her name to Ankhesenamun. Two tiny mummified daughters, both stillborn, were buried with him.

## A reign of restoration

He became king at about nine years old, and the government was in the hands of experienced officials, above all Ay, the "God's Father", and the general Horemheb. His *Restoration Stela*, set up at Karnak, describes temples fallen into ruin and gods who had turned away from Egypt, and announces the rebuilding of their shrines, the reopening of their priesthoods and the making of new cult statues. The new capital at Amarna was abandoned.

## How did he die?

He died around the age of nineteen, and the cause has been argued over for a century. X-rays in 1968 suggested a blow to the head and launched the theory that he was murdered; CT scans in 2005 found no sign of it, but showed a fracture of the left thigh, possibly suffered shortly before death. The 2010 study found a bone disorder in his left foot, many walking sticks among his grave goods, and DNA of the parasite that causes malaria. Wet paint and borrowed objects in the tomb suggest a hurried burial. Our documentary weighs all five clues: [How Did Tutankhamun Die?](/videos/how-did-tutankhamun-die/)

## After his death

A Hittite text records that an Egyptian queen, widowed and without a son, wrote to King Suppiluliuma asking for one of his sons as a husband. The prince sent, Zannanza, died on the way. Many historians identify the queen as Ankhesenamun. Ay succeeded Tutankhamun, and Horemheb later took the throne and had Tutankhamun's name cut from his monuments, including the Restoration Stela.

## The tomb and its treasures

KV62 is small for a king, and was probably adapted from a tomb intended for someone else. Inside four gilded shrines lay a quartzite sarcophagus and three nested coffins, the innermost made of solid gold. Over some ten years Howard Carter and his team recorded more than 5,000 objects, now gathered in the Grand Egyptian Museum. Among them were two trumpets, whose sound was broadcast to the world in 1939: see [The Banned Frequency of Tutankhamun's Trumpet](/videos/tutankhamun-trumpet-banned-frequency/). His mummy still lies in the tomb.
