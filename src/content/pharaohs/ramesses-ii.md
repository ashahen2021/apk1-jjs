The third king of the 19th Dynasty, Ramesses II ruled for 66 years and outlived many of his children, becoming, in later memory, the model of what a pharaoh should be. Nine later kings took his name.

## Prince and king

He was the son of Seti I, whose tomb [KV17](/tombs/kv17-seti-i/) is the finest in the Valley of the Kings, and as a young prince he joined his father on campaign and was given the rank of heir with his own household. He came to the throne around 1279 BCE, still in his twenties, and moved the main royal residence to a new city in the eastern Delta, **Pi-Ramesses**, "House of Ramesses", closer to Egypt's frontiers in the Levant.

## Kadesh and the first peace treaty

In his fifth year he marched north against the Hittite king Muwatalli II, who controlled the city of Kadesh on the Orontes in Syria. Misled by two Bedouin who claimed the Hittites were far away, the Egyptian army was caught strung out on the march and its second division was routed. Ramesses rallied his troops, reinforcements arrived, and the battle ended without either side taking the city. In his temples, at Abu Simbel, Luxor, Karnak, Abydos and the Ramesseum, it is told in poem and relief as a personal triumph.

The conflict led, in his 21st year, to a formal peace treaty with the Hittite king Hattusili III. Both versions survive: the Egyptian text carved at Karnak and the Ramesseum, and the Hittite text on a clay tablet from the Hittite capital, Hattusa. Thirteen years later Ramesses married a Hittite princess to seal the alliance.

## The great builder

No pharaoh built more, or carved his name on more, including over the names of earlier kings. Among his works are the two rock-cut temples of **[Abu Simbel](/monuments/abu-simbel/)** in Nubia, the Ramesseum, his mortuary temple on the west bank at Thebes, where the colossal fallen statue inspired Shelley's *Ozymandias*, and the huge hypostyle hall at Karnak, completed in his reign. The upper part of one of his statues from the Ramesseum is now in the British Museum: see the [Younger Memnon](/artifacts/younger-memnon/).

## Family

His chief queen in the early part of the reign was **Nefertari**, for whom he built the smaller temple at Abu Simbel and whose tomb, [QV66](/tombs/qv66-nefertari/), is the most beautiful in the Valley of the Queens. He had many other wives, including Isetnofret, and more than a hundred children, many of whom died before him; his thirteenth son, Merenptah, succeeded him. Several sons were buried in KV5, the largest tomb in the Valley of the Kings.

## Burial and mummy

Ramesses was buried in [KV7](/tombs/kv7-ramesses-ii/), now badly damaged by floods; you can see it near the entrance of the valley on the [Valley of the Kings 3D map](/tombs/valley-of-the-kings-map/). His mummy, moved by priests for safety some three centuries after his death, was found in 1881 in the royal cache at Deir el-Bahari. In 1976 it was flown to Paris for conservation, and it is now in the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization in Cairo.

## Where to see Ramesses II today

No pharaoh left more to visit. Some of the places where he can still be seen:

- **Abu Simbel**, Nubia: the four seated colossi and the sun alignment of February and October. See [Abu Simbel](/monuments/abu-simbel/).
- **The Ramesseum**, western Thebes: his memorial temple, where the broken granite colossus that inspired *Ozymandias* still lies.
- **Luxor Temple**: his pylon, seated colossi and the obelisk whose twin has stood in the Place de la Concorde in Paris since 1836.
- **The Grand Egyptian Museum**, Giza: the eleven-metre colossus that stood for decades in Cairo's Ramses Square now greets visitors in the atrium.
- **Mit Rahina (ancient Memphis)**: a fallen limestone colossus, displayed lying on its back in its own building.
- **The British Museum**, London: the [Younger Memnon](/artifacts/younger-memnon/), the upper part of a statue from the Ramesseum.
- **The National Museum of Egyptian Civilization**, Cairo: his mummy, moved there in the procession of royal mummies in 2021.
- **The Valley of the Kings**: his tomb, [KV7](/tombs/kv7-ramesses-ii/), and the vast tomb of his sons, KV5. Find both on the [Valley of the Kings 3D map](/tombs/valley-of-the-kings-map/).

## What we know, and what is still argued

- **Established:** he reigned for about 66 years, fought the Battle of Kadesh in his fifth year and made a written peace with the Hittites in his twenty-first.
- **Established:** Kadesh was not the victory his temples claim. The city stayed under Hittite control, and Hittite sources treat the campaign as their success.
- **Established:** he had more than a hundred sons and daughters; processions of them are carved in his temples.
- **No evidence:** that he was the pharaoh of the Exodus. The idea comes from the Bible's mention of a store-city called Raamses (Exodus 1:11); no Egyptian source records the events, and scholars do not agree on whether or when they happened.
