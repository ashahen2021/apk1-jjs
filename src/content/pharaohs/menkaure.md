Menkaure, a son of **[Khafre](/pharaohs/khafre/)**, built the third pyramid on the [Giza Plateau](/monuments/giza-plateau/). It is far smaller than the Great Pyramid, but its lowest sixteen courses were cased in granite, part of which was left with a rough, undressed surface: the casing was never finished.

## The third pyramid

As built it rose to about 65 metres on a base of a little over 100 metres a side, roughly a tenth of the volume of Khufu's pyramid. Its interior was reworked during construction: a first, higher passage was abandoned and a lower one cut, leading to a chamber with a ceiling carved to look like a barrel vault. In 1837 Richard Howard Vyse found there a decorated basalt sarcophagus. He shipped it to England, but the vessel carrying it, the *Beatrice*, sank off the coast of Spain in 1838, and the sarcophagus has never been recovered. Three small pyramids for queens stand to the south.

## Unfinished temples and celebrated sculpture

Menkaure seems to have died before his complex was complete. His successor Shepseskaf finished the temples quickly in mud brick, and later kings repaired them. Excavating the valley temple in 1908–10, George Reisner's expedition found celebrated sculpture, including the pair statue of the king with a queen, now in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, and a series of triads showing Menkaure with the goddess Hathor and the deity of a province of Egypt. Three complete triads are in Cairo and one in Boston. The faces, the king's broad shoulders and the queen's arm around his waist make them some of the finest Old Kingdom sculpture.

## The pious king

Greek tradition contrasted Menkaure, "Mycerinus", with his predecessors. Herodotus says he reopened the temples and judged his people fairly, and tells a tale of an oracle that gave him only six years to live, which he tried to double by turning night into day. Like the stories of Khufu, these are later legends, but they show that the smaller pyramid was remembered as the mark of a gentler king.

## The builders

The workers' town excavated by AERA south-east of the Sphinx was in use during the 4th Dynasty building programme, with bakeries, breweries, barracks-like galleries and evidence of large-scale cattle and fish supplies. Its excavators estimate a rotating workforce of thousands.
