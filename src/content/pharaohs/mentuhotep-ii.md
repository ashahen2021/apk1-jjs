A Theban ruler of the 11th Dynasty, Mentuhotep II completed the long war against the kings of Herakleopolis and reunited Egypt around 2055 BCE, opening the period now called the Middle Kingdom.

## Reuniting the Two Lands

After the end of the Old Kingdom, Egypt had split. Kings at Herakleopolis, south of modern Cairo, claimed rule over the north, while a family of rulers at Thebes, the city the Egyptians called Waset, gradually took control of the south. Mentuhotep II came to the throne in Thebes and, within the first two decades of his long reign of about fifty years, defeated Herakleopolis and brought the whole country under one king again.

The changes to his Horus name trace the progress. He began as *Seankhibtawy*, "he who makes the heart of the Two Lands live"; became *Netjerihedjet*, "divine one of the White Crown"; and finally took the name *Sematawy*, "he who unites the Two Lands". He restored a single administration, sent expeditions into Nubia and to the quarries of the Eastern Desert, and celebrated a *sed* jubilee.

## The soldiers' tomb

Near his temple, a rock-cut tomb excavated by Herbert Winlock in the 1920s held the bodies of about sixty soldiers, wrapped in linen and naturally preserved. Many had arrow wounds and injuries from stones thrown from above, as in the assault on a fortress; some had been left on the field long enough for birds to attack them. They are among the most direct evidence for the wars of reunification.

## A new kind of royal temple

His mortuary temple at **Deir el-Bahari**, on the west bank opposite Thebes, was unlike anything built before. A ramp led up to a terrace with colonnades, surrounding a central structure whose original form, a pyramid or a flat-topped mound, is still debated. Behind it, a hall of columns and a sanctuary were cut back into the cliff, and a long passage led to the king's burial chamber deep in the rock. Tombs for several of his wives and daughters were built into the platform.

In 1900 Howard Carter's horse stumbled into a hole in front of the temple. The shaft beneath, now called Bab el-Hosan, held a seated sandstone statue of the king wrapped in linen, his skin painted black, a colour of fertility and rebirth. The statue is now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.

Five centuries later the architects of the [Temple of Hatshepsut](/monuments/temple-of-hatshepsut/) built next door, and its terraces echo his design. Later kings remembered him as a founder: in the New Kingdom his statue was carried in festivals alongside those of Menes, the first king, and Ahmose, who drove out the Hyksos.
