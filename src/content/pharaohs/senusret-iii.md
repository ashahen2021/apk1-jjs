Senusret III, fifth king of the 12th Dynasty, campaigned repeatedly in Nubia and fixed Egypt's southern frontier at Semna, with a boundary stela warning his successors not to give up an inch of it. A system of fortresses along the Second Cataract controlled trade and movement on the river.

## The southern frontier

The Nile above the First Cataract at Aswan led to Nubia and its gold, ivory, ebony and cattle, and to the rising kingdom of Kush. In his eighth year Senusret III had a channel through the rocks of the First Cataract cleared and enlarged so that warships could pass. Campaigns followed in his eighth, tenth, sixteenth and nineteenth years.

At Semna, where the river runs through a narrow granite gorge, he set up a stela in year 16 declaring the frontier. Its text tells Nubians they may pass north only to trade, and addresses future kings: whoever holds the border is truly his son; whoever abandons it is not his son and was not born to him. A statue of the king was set up beside it to make the point.

## The fortresses

A chain of mud-brick fortresses guarded the stretch of river between Buhen and Semna: among them Buhen, Mirgissa, Shalfak, Uronarti, Kumma and Semna itself, with thick walls, bastions, ditches and fortified water stairs. Copies of reports sent between them, the *Semna Dispatches*, record small patrols that tracked the movements of Nubian groups in the desert. Most of these forts now lie under Lake Nasser, flooded when the Aswan High Dam was built in the 1960s.

## A changing state

Inside Egypt the reign marks a turning point. The great provincial governors, whose rich tombs had been a feature of the Middle Kingdom, disappear from the record during or soon after his reign, and administration was reorganised into departments run from the royal residence. Whether he removed the governors deliberately or the change was gradual is still debated.

## The king with a careworn face

His sculpture is famous for its heavy-lidded eyes, lined cheeks and large ears: a deliberate image of a king who sees and hears everything and carries the burden of rule. He built a mud-brick pyramid at Dahshur, cased in limestone, and a large complex at South Abydos that may have been his real burial. Beside the Dahshur pyramid, Jacques de Morgan found in 1894 the jewellery of royal women of the family, including gold pectorals bearing his name.

## A legend and a god

Later generations worshipped him as a god in Nubia; three centuries after his death [Thutmose III](/pharaohs/thutmose-iii/) rebuilt the temple at Semna in his honour. Greek writers told of a world-conquering king named Sesostris, a figure who seems to blend memories of Senusret III with other kings, including [Ramesses II](/pharaohs/ramesses-ii/). His son Amenemhat III, whose [pyramidion](/artifacts/pyramidion-of-amenemhat-iii/) is in Cairo, inherited a secure and prosperous kingdom. See the [12th Dynasty](/dynasties/12/).
