Daughter of Thutmose I and widow of Thutmose II, Hatshepsut acted as regent for her young stepson [Thutmose III](/pharaohs/thutmose-iii/) before taking the full titles of king. Her reign was prosperous and largely peaceful, focused on trade and building.

## From queen to king

As the chief wife of Thutmose II she held the title God's Wife of Amun, an office with its own estates and priestly role at Karnak. When her husband died, his heir Thutmose III, the son of a secondary wife, was still a child. Hatshepsut governed on his behalf, and within a few years she had been crowned king, with the throne name **Maatkara**. Thutmose III remained co-ruler, and official documents continued to be dated by his regnal years.

Her early images show her as a woman wearing royal regalia; later ones present her in the traditional form of a king, with a male body, kilt and false beard. Her inscriptions describe her as chosen by Amun from birth, and the reliefs of her temple show the god visiting her mother to father the future king.

## Punt, obelisks and temples

The best-known event of her reign is an expedition by sea to **Punt**, a land somewhere on the southern Red Sea coast, carved on the walls of her temple at Deir el-Bahari. The reliefs show the ships, the stilt houses of Punt, its ruler and his notably stout queen, and the return cargo of ebony, gold, animals and living incense trees in baskets, to be planted on the temple terraces.

At Karnak she raised a pair of granite obelisks from Aswan; one still stands, about 29.5 metres tall, the tallest ancient obelisk standing in Egypt. She also built the Red Chapel, a quartzite shrine for Amun's sacred boat, later dismantled and rebuilt in modern times from its blocks. Her temple at Deir el-Bahari, *Djeser-Djeseru*, "Holiest of Holies", rises in terraces against the cliffs beside the older temple of [Mentuhotep II](/pharaohs/mentuhotep-ii/): see the [Temple of Hatshepsut](/monuments/temple-of-hatshepsut/). Its chief official, the steward Senenmut, appears in more surviving statues than almost any other non-royal person of the period.

## Erasure and rediscovery

Some twenty years after her death, late in Thutmose III's reign, many of her images and names were cut from her monuments, and her obelisks at Karnak were walled in. Because this happened so long after she died, most Egyptologists now see it as a political act, perhaps to secure the succession of Thutmose III's son Amenhotep II, rather than as personal revenge.

She was buried in the Valley of the Kings, in tomb KV20, which she extended to hold the burial of her father too; you can find it on the [Valley of the Kings 3D map](/tombs/valley-of-the-kings-map/). In 2007 a mummy from the small tomb KV60 was identified as Hatshepsut, on the evidence of a tooth matching a box inscribed with her name. The identification is widely accepted, though not universally. Her portrait survives in sculpture such as the [seated statue of Hatshepsut](/artifacts/seated-statue-of-hatshepsut/) in New York.
