Crowned as a child and overshadowed for two decades by his stepmother [Hatshepsut](/pharaohs/hatshepsut/), Thutmose III went on to become Egypt's greatest military king. His victory at **Megiddo** opened Syria-Palestine to Egyptian control.

## The battle of Megiddo

In his 23rd regnal year, around 1457 BCE, shortly after Hatshepsut's death, a coalition of Canaanite and Syrian rulers led by the king of Kadesh gathered at Megiddo, in the north of modern Israel. Three routes led over the Carmel ridge to the city. According to his Annals, Thutmose III's officers urged the two longer, safer roads; the king chose the narrow Aruna pass, where the army had to march in single file, and came out unexpectedly before the enemy. The coalition was routed, but Egyptian soldiers stopped to loot the camp, and the survivors shut themselves in the city. Megiddo fell only after a siege of seven months.

## Seventeen campaigns

Over the next twenty years he led some seventeen campaigns in the Levant. In his 33rd year he crossed the Euphrates to strike at the kingdom of Mitanni, with boats carried overland on carts from the coast, and set up a stela beside the one his grandfather Thutmose I had placed there. The sons of conquered rulers were brought to Egypt to be raised at court and later sent back as loyal vassals. Tribute flowed to Thebes: gold, horses, chariots, timber and exotic animals and plants.

## The Annals and the Akh-menu

At Karnak he carved his Annals, a campaign record drawn from the daily journals kept by army scribes, on the walls around the sanctuary of Amun. He also built the **Akh-menu**, a festival hall whose columns imitate tent poles; in one of its rooms, often called the Botanical Garden, are carved the unfamiliar plants and animals brought back from Syria. Many of his obelisks now stand far from Egypt: the two "Cleopatra's Needles" in London and New York were made for him at Heliopolis, and others stand in Rome and Istanbul.

## Tomb and mummy

His tomb, **[KV34](/tombs/kv34-thutmose-iii/)**, is hidden high in a cleft of the cliffs at the southern end of the Valley of the Kings, as you can see on the [Valley of the Kings 3D map](/tombs/valley-of-the-kings-map/). Its oval burial chamber, shaped like a cartouche, contains the earliest complete version of the Amduat, the guide to the sun's journey through the twelve hours of the night, painted in a sketchy style like an unrolled papyrus. His mummy was found in 1881 in the royal cache at Deir el-Bahari.

He reigned for about 54 years, sharing the throne in his last years with his son Amenhotep II. See the [18th Dynasty](/dynasties/18/) for the family that built Egypt's empire.
